Votes and the Ground They Come From

Published November 22, 2026 by STARCITY, starcitylincoln.org. A first pass. It covers five general elections from 2012 to 2020 and eleven city votes and ballot questions from 2011 to 2025.

Summary

This paper asks one question: do precinct results in Lancaster County move with anything else in the public record? It sets the vote in 196 precincts beside the assessor's parcels and the Election Commissioner's registration counts. It describes places. It cannot say how any person voted, and nothing here shows that one thing causes another.

  1. The vote follows home value. In 2020, precincts in the lowest quarter by median home value gave the Democratic ticket 64.6% of the two-party vote. Precincts in the highest quarter gave it 47.1%.
  2. It follows the age of the housing just as closely. Precincts where the typical home was built before 1940 gave the Democratic ticket 72.0% in 2020. Where the typical home was built in 2000 or later, 48.3%.
  3. The gap is narrowing from the top. From 2012 to 2020 the lowest quarter moved +2.9 points toward the Democratic ticket and the highest quarter moved +10.7. The distance between them fell from 25 points to 17. Part of this is how early votes were counted; see the caution below.
  4. Party registration explains most of it. A precinct's share of registered Democrats tracks its 2020 result almost exactly (rank correlation 0.93). Home value and housing age are mostly a map of where each party's voters live.
  5. Fewer people are registered where the vote leans Democratic. Precincts with fewer registered voters per 100 residents leaned more Democratic in every election here (rank correlation -0.53 in 2020).
  6. Local taxes and bonds do not split by home value, and turnout does. Sales taxes and bonds drew about the same support in low-value and high-value precincts. In the May 2025 city election, turnout was 21.1% in the lowest-value quarter of precincts and 32.7% in the highest.
  7. Outside money cannot be tied to precincts. The state's filings say how much was spent for or against a candidate and on what, and not where the mail went. See the last section.

Three things stand out.

  • Local taxes and bonds do not split by home value. On the 2015 sales tax, the 2019 street tax, the 2019 stormwater bond and the 2025 street tax renewal, precincts with cheap homes and precincts with expensive homes voted nearly alike. The link to home value is close to zero each time.
  • Questions about who gets help do. Medicaid expansion in 2018 and the 2025 housing ordinance both passed everywhere, and both ran about 13 to 17 points higher in the lowest-value quarter than in the highest.
  • Mayor's races have come to follow home value. The link was zero in 2011, -0.25 in 2015 and -0.43 in 2019.

For 2011 to 2019 the result column is the official total, and the precinct columns leave out early votes, which the county reported only as one citywide line. That is why the 2019 street tax passed with 50.7% while every group of precincts shows under 50%: early voters carried it. Those years are also matched to today's precincts by name, and between 7 and 35 city precincts each year did not match. The 2025 reports count every ballot in its precinct, and their totals match the county's official results.

Turnout is ballots cast divided by registered voters, averaged across 174 Lincoln precincts. Citywide it was 24.6% in the primary. In the general election the highest-value quarter of precincts turned out at about one and a half times the rate of the lowest. A city election is decided by roughly a quarter of registered voters, and they are not spread evenly across the city.

Cautions that change how to read this

  • Early votes. In the 2012 and 2016 files, early votes are reported for the county as a whole, 31,500 and 34,675 votes, and are not in any precinct. In 2014, 2018 and 2020 there is no separate early line. So precinct shares in 2012 and 2016 leave out early voters, who leaned Democratic, and part of the shift toward 2020 is that difference in bookkeeping. The 2014 to 2018 governor comparison does not have this problem, and it shows the same pattern more mildly: the lowest quarter moved +3.4 points and the highest +5.2.
  • Places, not people. A precinct with expensive homes voting one way does not mean owners of expensive homes vote that way.
  • Today's values and registration, past votes. Home values are 2026 and registration is current. Both are set beside votes from as long as fourteen years ago.
  • Precincts change. Results are matched to today's precincts by name. Lines were redrawn after the 2020 census, so some older results sit on slightly different ground. Precincts with fewer than 30 homes or 100 registered voters are left out which removes precincts made up mostly of apartments, dorms or businesses.
  • Different races. A governor's race and a presidential race are different contests with different candidates.
  • Not yet checked line by line. County totals here are sums of the precinct file. They have not been compared with the county's certified canvass. This is on the verification page as unverified.

What is missing, and why

  • The 2023 mayor and council races by precinct. The Election Commissioner's page for May 2, 2023 has the citywide summary and turnout by precinct. A results-by-precinct report is not posted there. It is a public record and can be requested.
  • The November 2024 general election by precinct, including the ballot measures. The county's page posts a summary and turnout statistics. The November 2022 precinct report is posted and has not been read into this paper yet.
  • Where campaign mail and advertising went. The state does not collect it.
  • Renters. This map has no public count of renters by precinct, so homes stand in for households.
  • The county's precinct files themselves. The county's site turns away automated readers, so the reports were read in a web browser and the tables computed there. The steps are in the repository, but the files are not mirrored in it yet.

Votes and outside money

The campaign money paper found $1,068,502 in independent spending on the 2023 and 2025 city races. It is tempting to ask whether the precincts that got the mail voted differently. The public record cannot answer that. The filings give a date, an amount, a candidate, support or oppose, and a short description such as mailing or advertising. They do not say which households, precincts or districts were targeted. That is a gap worth asking the state to close, and it belongs on the data gaps page. The 2025 council table above is as far as the public record goes: spending for and against each candidate beside the result and the precinct pattern, shown together and nothing more.

Method

City votes: Lancaster County Election Commissioner precinct reports for 2011, 2015, 2016, 2018, 2019 and 2025, Lincoln precincts with at least 40 votes in the contest. General elections: OpenElections precinct files for Nebraska general elections, Lancaster County rows, top race each year. Precinct lines and registration: Lancaster County Election Commissioner, through City of Lincoln and Lancaster County GIS. Parcels: Lancaster County Assessor, 122,748 parcels, each assigned to the precinct containing a point inside it. Homes are improved residential parcels with floor area on record. Two-party share is Democratic votes divided by Democratic plus Republican votes. Correlations are Spearman rank correlations, unweighted, across precincts with at least 50 two-party votes.
